Progress dashboards can show too much information at once. The design challenge is to surface the signals a learner needs first while keeping deeper detail available when requested.

02 / RESEARCH + USERS

Prioritize the questions that matter.

The research plan asks what learners need to know at a glance, which signals create confusion, and how dashboard information should behave on smaller screens.

Interview questions
Understand goals, terminology, and progress expectations.
Task observation
Observe scanning, drilling into a subject, and returning.
Pain-point mapping
Capture information overload and unclear hierarchy.
Constraint review
Review responsive, accessibility, and connectivity needs.
03 / IA + FLOW

Reveal detail progressively.

Surface overall progress first, then subjects and chapters, with recent activity and deeper details available without competing with the primary task.

05 / TEST + IMPROVE

Validate scanning and comprehension.

Testing should examine whether users can understand the dashboard hierarchy, find a subject, interpret progress, and navigate with keyboard and responsive layouts.

Usability
Test representative scan-and-find tasks.
Iteration
Document changes, rationale, and unresolved questions.
Accessibility
Check focus, labels, contrast, hierarchy, and non-color-only states.
Privacy
Keep learner data collection minimal and transparent.
VALIDATION STATUS
No fabricated usability results are presented. Findings will be added after actual testing.
06 / OUTCOME

Define success before claiming it.

Success would mean learners can quickly understand their current study context and reach useful detail without visual overload. The next evidence milestone is task-based usability testing.
